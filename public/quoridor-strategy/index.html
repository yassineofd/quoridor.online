Quoridor strategy

How to think about the race, when walls are worth it, and which mistakes to avoid.

On this page

Efficient pathfinding

Every decision in Quoridor comes down to one comparison: how many moves do I need to win, and how many does my opponent need? Count the shortest route for each pawn, taking walls and possible jumps into account.

If you're ahead on that count, moving usually keeps you ahead. If you're behind, you need a wall that changes the count in your favor.

Offensive and defensive wall placement

A wall is an offensive tool when it lengthens your opponent's route. It's defensive when it protects your own path, for example by occupying a groove your opponent could use to cut across your route.

  • The best walls add several steps, not one. Look for spots where a pawn must backtrack along a corridor.
  • Walls work best together. Extending an existing wall or using the board edge can turn a small barrier into a long detour.
  • Remember that your opponent can walk around, so aim for walls that force the longest walk-around.

Wall management

You start with 10 walls and they're gone for good once placed. Treat them as a budget:

  • Don't spend walls early just because you can.
  • Running out of walls leaves you unable to respond if your opponent starts to pull ahead.
  • Walls your opponent hasn't used are a threat: you must always consider what they could do next turn.

Timing decisions

A wall is more valuable when it comes at the right moment. Placing it after your opponent has committed to a route often costs them more than placing it before. On the other hand, waiting too long can mean a good wall spot disappears or the game is already lost.

A useful habit: before placing any wall, ask whether you could place the same wall next turn at no extra cost. If you can, take the step now instead.

Common mistakes

  • Walling for one step. A wall that adds a single step usually costs you a turn and a wall for no net gain.
  • Ignoring your own path. Strong walls can be turned against you by a later wall.
  • Using walls too early. Few walls left means few options late in the game.
  • Forgetting jumps. The opponent's pawn can change where your path runs, so recount often.
  • Never moving. Walls slow your opponent but only moving wins the game.

Beginner and advanced considerations

If you're a beginner

  • Learn to count both routes before every move.
  • Prefer moving when you're ahead.
  • Practice spotting walls that add three or more steps.

As you improve

  • Plan sequences of walls that combine into long corridors.
  • Think about where the opponent's pawn will be, not only where it is.
  • Use pawn positions to create jump or block opportunities.

These are general rules of thumb rather than proven optimal play. Test them against real opponents.
